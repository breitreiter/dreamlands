Black water sucks at your boots with each step. Biting flies are constant, not swarming, just present. The trail has vanished under a flat carpet of duckweed, no break in it. You navigate by the cypress moss on the trees and the current in the channels.

The smell of smoke arrives before any light is visible. You round a curtain of hanging moss and find a stilt-shack patched with bark, river-reed, and grey sailcloth. A dugout canoe is tethered below, and animal bones hang from the eaves on cords of sinew, clicking in a breeze.

A cookfire burns in a ring of river stones on the listing porch. A blackened pot steams above it, and the smell of herbs and root vegetables cuts through the swamp-stink, something warm and undeniably good.

An old woman sits beside the fire as though she has been waiting. Her face is deeply weathered, white hair threaded with feathers and small bones. Her eyes are pale, clear, and amused. Three teeth are missing, and the remaining ones are very white. She wears layered mud-stained clothes.

She stirs the pot with a long spoon, tapping it once against the rim. "Eat," she says, offering a wooden bowl. The steam carries the smell of herbs and root vegetables, cutting through the swamp-stink. "Channels'll be six feet deep by dark. Tide'll drown the way back." Her pale eyes are clear, almost amused.

Her eyes follow the lines of your pack, the worn leather of your boots, the shape of what you carry. She does not look away, her gaze moving slow and deliberate. "Not many visitors last out here," she says, the spoon tapping once against the pot's rim.

A scrape echoes from inside the shack, followed by a heavy settling weight. The woman does not look back toward the sound.
