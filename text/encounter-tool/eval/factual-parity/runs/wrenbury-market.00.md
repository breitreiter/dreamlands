You arrive at the Wrenbury market battlefield relic sale. At the entrance, a man stands beside a portable clay oven, using a flat paddle to hand off meat pies wrapped in squares of waxed cloth. Next to him, a woman sells small beer from a worn cask, the wood bowed and the stave-rings dark with use. The smell of meat pies and warm beer hangs over the market.

Past the food stalls, blankets spread on the ground display battle relics. One blanket holds a grid of rust-pitted spearheads and arrowpoints on dark cloth, each piece labeled with a handwritten card noting origin and unit. Next to it, another blanket bears buttons, buckles, and a dented canteen with something scratched into the side that might be a name, no cards for these.

One stall breaks the pattern entirely. Here, Revathi tile fragments are sorted by color, blues, ochres, and an unusual deep green not found in imperial glazework. Each shard is curved, the glaze still intact, clearly old. They don't belong on a battlefield relic table, and no one offers an explanation for their presence.

At the end of the row, a stall holds military histories and map prints. The bookseller watches every hand that moves near his stock, his face set into permanent watch.

Raised voices carry over the market's general noise. A young woman leans over a blanket at a seated vendor, her back rigid. She follows her first shout with a composure that doesn't match the tension in her shoulders. The vendor leans back, palms up, showing empty hands. Bystanders have slowed to watch, their movement turning to stillness as they take in the scene.

The woman's eyes catch on the ring on your hand. She holds your gaze, her posture still rigid, not calling out yet. The implicit offer hangs in the air: walk over, and you're no longer a tourist.
