You arrive at the Wrenbury market, a battlefield relic market held in the village. The air smells of meat pies and warm beer. At the entrance, a vendor uses a portable clay oven and a flat paddle to hand off pies wrapped in squares of waxed cloth. Next to him, a woman sells small beer from a worn cask, its stave-rings dark and the wood bowed.

Past the food, blanket stalls display battle relics. One blanket holds a grid of rust-brown, pitted spearheads and arrowpoints on dark cloth, each piece labeled with a neat handwritten card noting its origin and unit. On the next blanket, a dented canteen lies with buttons and buckles, something scratched into its side that might be a name, but none of these items have cards.

One stall breaks the pattern entirely. It holds Revathi tile fragments sorted by color, blues, ochres, and a deep green not found in imperial glazework. Each shard is curved with intact glaze, clearly genuine and old, though they are from the wrong battlefield and century.

At the end of the row, a bookseller watches his stock with the face of a man who has spent years telling people not to touch things. His eyes are on every hand that moves near his military histories and map prints.

Past him, raised voices carry over the market noise. A young woman leans over a blanket at a seated vendor, her posture rigid after a shout. He leans back, palms up. Bystanders have slowed to watch. The woman clocks the ring on your finger, you're a Guild arbiter, and locals recognize it. She holds your gaze, not calling out, but the implicit offer is clear: walk over and you're no longer a tourist.
