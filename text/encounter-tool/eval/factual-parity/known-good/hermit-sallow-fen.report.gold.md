You smell the smoke before you see the light.

Deep in the fen: black water sucking at your boots, the ceaseless drone of biting flies, air so thick with rot it coats the back of your throat. The trail, such as it was, vanished yesterday beneath a carpet of duckweed. Since then you have navigated by the moss on the cypress knees and the slow pull of the current beneath the surface, trusting that the deeper channels lead inland toward high ground and the frontier beyond.

You had not expected to find anyone living here. Nothing does, that doesn't bite or sting or drag itself through the muck.

Yet there it is: a smudge of smoke above the treeline, yellow-grey against the perpetual overcast, and beneath it, as you round a curtain of hanging moss, a dwelling. A shack built on stilts above the waterline, patched with bark and river-reed and what looks like sailcloth long since gone to grey. Animal bones hang from the eaves on cords of sinew, clicking softly in a breeze you cannot feel. A dugout canoe is tethered to one stilt. On the listing porch a cookfire burns in a ring of river stones, and over it a blackened pot sends up steam that cuts through the swamp-stink: herbs, root vegetables, something warm.

The woman sits beside the fire as though she has been waiting.

She is old, her face deeply lined, her hair a white tangle threaded with feathers and what might be small bones. Her clothes are layered and shapeless, stained with mud and tannin. She watches you with pale, clear eyes. Amused.

"Sit," she says.

She does not rise. She does not reach for a weapon. She stirs the pot with a wooden spoon and the smell intensifies, rich and savory. Your stomach clenches; it has been too long since you had a warm meal.

"You'll want to eat before the water rises," she says, almost cheerful. "Moon's pulling the fen tonight. The channels you walked this morning will be six feet deep by dark." She taps the spoon against the pot's rim. "If you know the way through, you'll manage fine. If you don't..." She shrugs.

Her pale eyes drift to your pack, your boots, the shape of what you carry. She smiles. Three teeth are missing. The rest are very white.

"I don't get visitors," she says. "Not ones who last."

Something moves in the shack behind her. A scrape, a settling weight. She does not look back at it.
