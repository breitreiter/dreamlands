The market smells of meat pies and warm beer. A vendor works a portable clay oven, pulling pies with a flat paddle and handing them off in squares of waxed cloth. Next to him, a woman fills cups of small beer from a worn cask. Neither is selling anything to do with the battle; they're selling lunch.

The blankets start past the food. One displays a careful grid of spearheads and arrowpoints, all pitted and brown with rust, laid out on dark cloth. The seller has written little cards in neat handwriting, labeling each piece by origin and unit. The next blanket is more personal: buttons, buckles, a dented canteen with something scratched into the side that might be a name. No cards for these.

One stall breaks the pattern: Revathi tile fragments, arranged by color, each a curved shard of deep glaze — blues and ochres and a green you don't see in imperial work. Genuine, clearly. The wrong battlefield and the wrong century, but the seller doesn't seem troubled by it.

At the end of the row, a stall stacked with military histories and map prints. The bookseller sits behind them, his face set into a permanent watchfulness. He watches every hand that comes near his stock.

Past him, raised voices.

A young woman leans across a blanket at a seated vendor, her composure rigid and her voice tight, with the look of someone who has already shouted once and is trying not to again. He leans back, palms up, weathering it. A few people have slowed to watch, finding reasons to linger nearby.

She glances at you, and her eyes catch on your hand — your ring. She doesn't call out, not yet, but she holds your gaze. If you walk over there, you stop being a tourist and become a Guild arbiter.
