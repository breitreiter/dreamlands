You are lost in the trackless fen. The black water sucks at your boots with each step, and biting flies are a constant presence. The trail has vanished under a flat carpet of duckweed, leaving no break. You navigate by the cypress moss on the trees and the current in the channels.

You smell smoke before you see any light. Rounding a curtain of hanging moss, you find a stilt-shack patched with bark, river-reed, and sailcloth faded to grey. A dugout canoe is tethered below, and animal bones hang from the eaves on cords of sinew, clicking in a faint breeze. On the listing porch, a cookfire burns in a ring of river stones. A blackened pot steams over it, the smell of herbs and root vegetables cutting through the swamp-stink. The smell from the pot is undeniably good, and your stomach clenches.

An old woman sits beside the fire as though she has been waiting. Her face is deeply weathered, and her white, tangled hair is threaded with feathers and what look like small bones. Her pale, clear eyes are amused, and three teeth are missing from her layered, mud-stained clothes. She taps a spoon once against the pot's rim.

"Food's warm," she says, her tone almost cheerful. "The channels will be six feet deep by dark. The tide'll drown your way back. Best eat while you can."

Her gaze moves to your pack, your boots, and the shape of what you carry. She does not rise or reach for anything. "Not many visitors last," she notes.

Something massive shifts inside the shack behind her, a scrape, then a settling weight. She does not look back toward the sound.
