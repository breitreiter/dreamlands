# The Hermitage
## Forest Biome Encounter — Incommensurable Values

### Core Theme
The costs and contradictions of freedom. A pacifist community built by former warriors confronts the discovery that its peace is not self-sustaining, and that every solution to that problem destroys the thing it's trying to save.

---

### Setting

A small hermitage deep in the forest, home to 12–14 people. The community is the remnant of Brother Aldous's old imperial command — the soldiers who deserted with him after his blinding, followed him into the forest, and spent the following decades learning how to be something other than soldiers. About half the residents are capable of physical labor (farming, maintenance); the rest are elderly or young — the old cohort has aged in place, and some paired off and raised children here. The hermitage survives by being remote, poor, and uninteresting to the outside world.

### The Deserters

A squad of 3–5 imperial deserters. They were rotated into the plains wildlands — a tour through the skeletonized campaign infrastructure left after the Retrenchment, and a posting reserved for soldiers who'd angered someone powerful or were too difficult to serve with. Their duty was to hold a half-collapsed fort and run patrols through abandoned depots that the empire no longer has any real claim to, resenting the scavenger bands that squatted in the surrounding ruins. These soldiers eventually deserted and pushed into the forest, lacking any survival skills beyond military ones.

They had heard rumors of the hermitage — a place where warriors go to lay down their swords — and misunderstood its function. They approached seeking shelter. The hermitage's one threshold rule is that visitors must leave weapons at the gate. The deserters, conditioned by imperial service to read disarmament as a prelude to execution, refused. They were turned away.

They returned later and occupied a barn. No arson, no pitched battle — they simply claimed the space. That was a month ago.

### The Situation at Arrival

The player follows no smoke or signs of disaster. They arrive to find a functioning but deeply tense community. The deserters occupy the barn. The monks won't look in that direction. An uneasy détente has calcified over four weeks of minor incidents: a borrowed tool not returned, a condescending remark about transformation, a drunk soldier pissing on the herb garden (maybe deliberately, maybe not — the ambiguity is itself a grievance).

The power dynamic is coercive but unspoken. The deserters haven't committed violence. They're just armed, present, and consuming resources. The monks — former warriors themselves — recognize the implicit threat in a hand resting on a pommel, in the body language of men who know how to use force. The monks are afraid, but not of the soldiers. They're afraid of themselves — afraid that their old instincts are waking up, that the transformation they thought was complete is eroding day by day.

The hospitality relationship was never established. The deserters were never received as guests (they refused the threshold terms). The monks never extended full welcome. Both groups occupy a liminal non-status: squatters who think of themselves as neighbors, owners who think of themselves as besieged.

### The Inciting Incident

The imperial deserters return to the hermitage laden with smoked meats and bags of grain, robbed from a travelling merchant. It is perverse hospitality — the deserters recognize they've been freeloading and are trying to make it right. They can't farm, gather, or tend animals. But they can lay and execute an ambush. By their own moral framework, this is a genuine and selfless contribution.

The food sits in the yard. Nobody eats it. Nobody throws it away. The deserters are confused, then angry. The monks can't touch it without becoming fences for stolen goods. And a merchant was robbed near a known settlement — word will spread, and the people who come looking won't distinguish between armed soldiers in the barn and unarmed monks in the chapel.

### Three Factions

**The Imperial Deserters.** Triumphant, wanting their contribution recognized. They use violence instrumentally, without much reflection — it's a tool, the way it's always been. They are not cruel, just unexamined. Their impulse to contribute is real. Their desire to belong is real. They are reaching toward reciprocity in the only language they speak.

**The Devoted.** The hermitage's surviving founders and their children — what's left of Aldous's old command, the soldiers who walked out of the wildlands with him and spent the following decades learning how to be something other than soldiers. They believe the imperials in the barn can be converted through ministry and patience because they themselves were those men once. They see the stolen-food gesture as a teachable moment: these men *wanted* to give, they just don't yet know how to give without taking. They maintain that the community's principles — nonviolence, hospitality, transformation — are non-negotiable, and that abandoning them to solve a crisis is abandoning the community itself. Brother Osric speaks for them with the player: a former corporal under Aldous's command, the easiest of the devoted for an outsider to talk to, and the one who can tell the story Aldous will never tell.

**The Kesharat Deserters.** A small, tight-knit group of former Kesharat soldiers who deserted together after witnessing what their superiors were doing and how they were being used. They trust each other because they served together — this is a fire team, not a conspiracy of strangers. They have been living among the devoted, performing the practices, perhaps half-believing, but their actual moral framework was never categorical nonviolence. They rejected *unjust* violence. They have very high standards for what justifies force, and protecting this community now meets that standard.

They are planning to quietly kill the imperial deserters. They have a timeline. Their leader has offered to own the consequences — to leave the hermitage afterward if that's what the community demands. This is not a bluff. This is someone who understands that you can do a necessary thing and be ruined by it, and who is volunteering to be the community's sin-eater.

The Kesharat have already done this exact moral calculus once before, when they deserted. They looked at a corrupt system, decided their conscience outweighed their orders, and acted. Now they see the same pattern: an institution they love, led by people who prioritize principle over survival, failing to protect itself. They know what happens next because they've *seen* it.

### Key Characters

#### Brother Aldous — The Devoted Founder
*The Carradine.*

Older, blind, founded the hermitage decades ago. A presence more than a participant now. The hermitage shapes itself around him — new arrivals see him before they meet him, a still figure at the edge of the garden, in the chapel, on the porch with his face turned toward whatever is happening in the yard. He does not often speak. When he does, it is usually in answer, and briefly.

Before the hermitage, Aldous led the imperial effort to take the plains fort network back after the Retrenchment. Not through force — his legions were outnumbered and outgunned from the first week — but through patience, misdirection, and a preternatural feel for what the Iron would and would not do. He took forts back one at a time. He held them with fewer men than should have been possible. For a decade the empire believed the Retrenchment might be reversed. Then a heat beam took his sight near a signal tower on the wildlands edge, and the empire, which had been running on him as a one-man institution, discovered it had no one to replace him with. Command passed sideways, then stalled. His gains decayed. Rotated platoons and scavenger bands now squat in the forts he bled to take. Edric's squad — imperial deserters holed up in a barn a hundred miles east of the line — is the latest symptom of the decay.

He knows this. He has made his peace with it in the only way a man like him can: by refusing to re-enter the world that required him. His blindness, which began as catastrophe, became the instrument of his withdrawal. He does not tell his own story. The devoted know it because they lived it with him; everyone else knows it, if at all, from someone else.

He moves through the hermitage with absolute confidence. Every flagstone, every doorframe, every garden row by feel and memory. The place is an extension of his body. He *knows* the deserters are in the barn the same way he knows where his own hand is.

He is serene but not soft. When a visitor says the wrong thing, something *shifts* — a stillness that's different from his usual stillness, and a nearby monk changes the subject. The gentleness is not the absence of violence but its constant active suppression.

He won't fight. He will die in the yard before he picks up a weapon. Not because he's a pacifist the way a civilian is — because he knows exactly what he'd do with a weapon and he made a vow to never be that person again.

Before the hermitage, Aldous went by a different name — *the Scarecrow* — still carrying weight among old wildlands veterans and anyone who served on the plains line. The devoted never use it. He hasn't heard it in decades.

When he engages the player, it is usually to ask a question the player didn't know they'd been asked. *"If you stop the killing tonight, what have you saved?"* *"When you leave this place, who do you carry with you?"* He will not explain what he means. He will not repeat himself. He will answer follow-up questions with other questions, or with silence, or with an apparent change of subject that only afterward resolves into a response. He will never ask the player to fight on the hermitage's behalf. He will also never tell the player not to. What the player does is the player's karma.

#### Meilin — The Kesharat Leader
*The Momoa.*

Huge. Takes up space without meaning to, ducks through doorways, has broken at least two of the hermitage's chairs. Laughs too loud and too often. Gives people nicknames within minutes. Physically incapable of being in a room without touching someone — a hand on a shoulder, a playful shove, ruffling a kid's hair. If she likes you, you know immediately. If she doesn't, you also know immediately.

She is the most dangerous person in this encounter by a significant margin.

The goofiness is real — not a mask over the soldier. But the soldier is also real, and the transition between them is instantaneous and absolute. One moment she's laughing about the barn cat stealing the imperials' dried fish. The next moment the player mentions the Kesharat leadership and her face goes flat and her eyes go somewhere else and the temperature drops. Then it's over and she's laughing again and you're not sure it happened.

She deserted the Kesharat with her unit because she saw what the system was doing and couldn't square it. She didn't agonize about it. She's not a philosopher. She saw something wrong, said "we're leaving," and everyone she trusted followed. Moral instinct, immediate action, deal with consequences later. It's what makes her an effective leader and what makes her dangerous right now.

Her plan to kill the imperial deserters isn't emotional. She's not angry at them. She's genuinely fond of one or two, the way you might be fond of a dog that bites. But she's done the calculation: these men cannot stop being what they are, the hermitage cannot survive what they attract, and the devoted are incapable of solving this because their principles prevent them from even seeing it clearly. She will do it at night, cleanly, and she will hate it, and she will leave afterward and never talk about it.

Her relationship with Aldous is the emotional core of the Kesharat subplot. She loves him. She genuinely believes he saved her life by giving her a place to be something other than a soldier. And she is going to betray everything he built because she thinks it's the only way to save it. She cannot explain this to him because the explanation would break his heart, and she'd rather be a murderer than break his heart. Enormous capacity for love, enormous capacity for violence, and a complete inability to see the contradiction.

#### Edric — The Imperial Leader
*The Statham.*

Compact, hard, economical. Doesn't waste words or motion. Sits where he can see the door. When he talks, he's watching your hands. This isn't paranoia — it's professional habit so deeply ingrained it's become personality. He was good at his job. Probably the best soldier in whatever garrison they stuck him in, which is partly why he's here — competent men in punishment postings either break or become local kings. Edric was becoming the latter, and his commanding officer shipped him further out.

His defining quality is more unsettling than open aggression: *pleasantness*. He can be charming. He can compliment the monks' bread with complete sincerity. But there is always something behind it — not malice, but an awareness of violence as a live option in any interaction. He's pleasant the way a man is pleasant when he knows he could end the conversation differently and is choosing not to. The monks feel this. The Kesharat recognize it immediately because they've served with men like this.

He is not evil. He is a product of a system that took a young man, trained him to solve problems with force, stationed him in the worst posting available, and discarded him. He has no survival skills because the legion never taught him any — why would they? He was a weapon, and weapons don't forage. When his squad deserted, they had no framework for existing outside military structure. They went hungry because nobody told them how to eat.

The robbery was his idea and he's not ashamed of it. He saw a problem and executed a solution. The method is not, in his mind, a moral failing. It's a skill set. He genuinely expected gratitude, and the monks' refusal to eat the food confuses and offends him.

On the conversion path, Edric is the hardest sell and the most rewarding one. He's not watching the monks' morning practice with quiet longing. He's watching it the way he'd watch an opposing force: assessing capabilities, noting patterns, looking for weaknesses. But there might be one moment — the player has to find it — where the assessment fails. Where he sees something he can't categorize as tactical. The conversion path isn't about convincing Edric that nonviolence is morally right. It's about getting him to *want something he doesn't have a name for*.

**Note:** One of Edric's soldiers sits apart from the others, quieter, watching the monks through the barn door with something other than tactical assessment. This soldier is the player's thread for the conversion path — the one Edric doesn't realize he's already losing.

### Scene Structure: The Pivotal Visit

The encounter's most consequential decision is not a skill check — it's who the player brings when they go to talk to Edric's squad.

#### The Player Goes Alone

Dead end, but not a lockout. Edric has no frame for the player — just another armed traveler, a civilian with opinions. He'll lay out his grievance like a briefing: they arrived, asked for shelter, were told to disarm (insane), took the barn (empty anyway), kept to themselves, then solved the community's supply problem at personal risk, and nobody will eat the food. What do these people *want?*

The player can try to explain the monks' position. Edric will listen politely and dismiss it: "So they'd rather starve correctly than eat well. I've met officers like that." He's pattern-matching the monks onto the worst imperial bureaucrats he served under — people who cared more about protocol than outcomes.

The value of this conversation is *information*. The player learns how Edric thinks, what he respects, what he dismisses. They learn he's not malicious, that he sees himself as the reasonable party, and that no amount of talking will move him because the player hasn't established standing in any language Edric recognizes. He needs either someone to look *up* to or someone to look *across* at. The player alone is someone he looks past.

The player can also spot the quiet soldier watching the monks — the conversion path's thread — even if they can't act on it yet.

#### The Player Brings Brother Osric → Opens the Conversion Path

Brother Osric is a former corporal from Aldous's old command — one of the soldiers who walked out of the wildlands with him after the reconquest collapsed. Edric clocks him on sight: not by face, but by *type*. The way he stands. The old habits that haven't quite left him. The place on his belt where a scabbard used to hang and still shapes his posture.

For the first minute, Edric treats him as another civilian. Then Osric starts talking — not about the hermitage, not about nonviolence, but about the line. The forts. The relief columns that never came. Officers who spent men like coin. Told flat, like a report. *"I served under the Scarecrow during the reconquest. Eventually we saw how the thing was being run. We walked out."*

He never says Aldous is the Scarecrow. He doesn't have to. Edric's men shift, quietly — one of them knows the name from a grandfather's stories; another has heard it in a recruiting barracks, always as past tense. Edric is already recalibrating. The old blind monk in the chapel is not a gentle fool who chose peace because he couldn't hack it. He is a man who was very good at the work, looked at what the work had become, and took an entire command with him when he walked.

The reframing works within Edric's value system in a different key than raw strength. Osric is not more dangerous than Edric. He is *a version of Edric* — the version that got out. The wildest claim the hermitage makes — that a soldier can put the work down and become something else — is standing in front of Edric in sandals, unremarkable and unashamed, telling a story about his former commander without ever quite naming him. Edric doesn't convert in this scene. But the thing he cannot unsee is that the path is real, that someone exactly like him has walked it, and that it didn't end in weakness. For Edric, this is a crack in the foundation.

#### The Player Brings Meilin → Opens the Pragmatic Path (Near-Failure State)

The conversation starts with the player talking. Meilin hangs back, arms crossed, letting the PC run their diplomatic approach. Edric does his pleasant-but-guarded thing, giving nothing.

Then Meilin says something offhand — a tactical observation about the barn's sightlines, or a comment about how Edric's men have arranged their gear. Something that signals: *I see what you've set up here. It's competent, but your fallback position is wrong.* Not a threat. Professional critique.

Edric's demeanor shifts. Not warmer — *engaged*. For the first time in a month, someone is speaking his language. He responds with a tactical observation. Meilin counters. They're talking about the barn's defensibility, then the hermitage's perimeter, then the forest approaches. The player realizes they're not mediating anymore. They're listening to two professionals plan a defense.

The body language changes — Meilin uncrosses her arms, Edric stops watching the door. They're facing each other like equals. The player is physically outside the conversation, a minor figure with no ability to alter the trajectory.

Meilin doesn't realize what's happening either. She came in to help the player and she's being pulled back into the thing she does best. Every tactical observation is a step away from the hermitage's principles and a step toward the pragmatic resolution. She's not betraying Aldous on purpose. She's just being who she actually is in the presence of someone who recognizes it. And it feels *good* — better than morning prayers, better than gardening, better than pretending she's at peace when she's been running threat assessments in her head every day since the deserters arrived.

The player's Negotiation check to steer the conversation back should get harder with each exchange, or at some point lock out entirely. They brought the wrong person into this room and now the encounter's trajectory has changed.

### The Player's Two Tracks

**Immediate Resolution:** Resolve the cohabitation crisis and the threat from the robbed merchant's people before the Kesharat act on their timeline.

**Stability:** Help the community process what this month has revealed about itself. Even after the immediate crisis is resolved, the monks now know their peace is fragile, their transformations may be incomplete, and their principles may be insufficient to sustain the community without outside intervention. The player's influence on this reckoning determines what the hermitage becomes.

### Three Resolutions

#### 1. Violence
*The Kesharat act.*

The imperial deserters are killed. The Kesharat are banished — either by community demand or by their own prior agreement. The devoted are left in a building that is now a crime scene, spiritually if not literally.

The community got what it wanted: the threat removed, the principles technically unbroken (the devoted didn't do the violence). But everyone knows the peace was *defended* by force, by people who shared their meals and prayers and were apparently never truly converted. The devout will survive, but they will never again believe their peace is self-sustaining. They needed warriors, and they sent those warriors away, and that contradiction will haunt them.

The Kesharat are back in the forest — a small armed band with no home. They are now exactly what the imperial deserters were a month ago. The cycle restarts.

**Thematic answer:** Freedom is fragile. It cannot defend itself without ceasing to be what it is. The people willing to defend it must be expelled because they represent everything it defines itself against.

**Variant 1a: Intentional.** The PC signs onto Meilin's plan and shares authorship of it. They help set the timing, the approach, the watch. They are there when it happens, and they own their presence. Meilin doesn't ask — she'd offered to carry it alone — but the PC insists, because you don't let someone be ruined by work you agreed with. It is done at night, cleanly, and it is ugly even when nothing goes wrong. When morning comes, the PC and the Kesharat leave together. No banishment scene, no argument. Aldous is on the porch. He knows where they are going and who is going with them. He does not speak.

**Variant 1b: Chaos Level 99.** The PC has not landed on a path. They've poked at the edges, been sympathetic, been uncertain, let the clock run. On the night Meilin had chosen, the Kesharat produce swords and armor — from under floorboards, from a cache in the woods, from wherever. They have been hedging all along. Maybe Meilin was never quite sure she wouldn't need to fight something other than her squad when this was over. The execution doesn't execute. Someone hears a footfall too early, or someone loses their nerve for an eyeblink, and a pitched fight breaks out in the yard. Chairs break. A brazier is kicked over. Both sides take casualties. One of the devoted takes a wound that will never quite heal. Aldous comes out when the noise stops and tells the Kesharat to leave. They do. Meilin is the last through the gate, and she does not look back, because she cannot, because she loves him.

#### 2. Conversion
*The hardest path. The miracle ending.*

The player reaches the imperial deserter closest to turning — the one who's been watching the monks' morning practice, who felt something when they brought the food back and can't name it. Brother Osric shares his story. The player brokers a genuine moment of transformation, or at least the beginning of one.

The player writes or stamps a letter resolving the merchant robbery — putting their own name and reputation behind the guarantee. The Kesharat stand down, accepting that their plan, however justified, was not the only option. The imperials who convert stay. Others may drift away as the group's cohesion breaks.

There is hard work ahead. The devoted must forgive in the grinding daily reality of sharing meals with people who robbed a merchant and squatted in their barn. The Kesharat must accept that competent violence was unnecessary — a difficult thing to sit with when it was probably the right call if the player hadn't shown up. The player's letter is out in the world with their seal on it, tying them to the community's future.

**Thematic answer:** Freedom can absorb its contradictions, but only through genuine transformation, and transformation requires someone to take an enormous risk on people who haven't earned it yet. It cannot be guaranteed. It can only be offered.

**Mechanical note:** This path requires multiple sequential checks and is the most demanding. The player must identify the right imperial, facilitate the conversion, resolve the merchant problem, and convince the Kesharat to stand down before their deadline — all without dropping a plate.

#### 3. Pragmatism
*The easiest path to reach. The most quietly horrifying on reflection.*

The player brokers a conversation between the Kesharat leader and the imperial commanding officer. Two veterans find common ground. The Kesharat re-arm. The imperials are absorbed into the community but only agree to stop doing banditry. Aldous leaves the hermitage on foot the same evening, the moment he understands what has been agreed to. He says goodbye to no one. The experiment he built across decades — the claim that violence can be set down — turned out to have been a hallucination held together by his presence, and now his own people have voted to keep the building and hollow it out. He cannot stay to watch it. No one knows where he is going or what he will do next. Some of the devoted follow him within the hour. Others stay, accepting the pragmatic reality — a monastery with guards is better than a monastery that doesn't exist.

The hermitage now has a spiritual class and a warrior class. The warriors have accepted a rule (no banditry), which means someone must enforce the rule, which means there's authority. The Kesharat hold a monopoly on organized violence. The devoted provide spiritual legitimacy. This is a government. This is literally the social contract. The player has, in a single encounter, recapitulated the entire history of how intentional communities become institutions become kingdoms.

**Thematic answer:** Freedom is traded, rationally and voluntarily, for security. It is always traded this way. The ease of the exchange is the point — building a state is easy. That's why it keeps happening.

### Design Notes

- The stolen food should remain in the yard for the duration of the encounter, rotting slowly, a physical reminder of the unresolved question. Nobody eats it. Nobody throws it away.
- The Kesharat timeline is a real mechanical deadline, not a narrative beat. Their leader's patience is the encounter's clock.
- The violence resolution can function as the failure state for the other two paths — if the player tries conversion and can't close it, or tries pragmatism and the Kesharat move first.
- The two mirrors to emphasize: (a) the Kesharat looking at the imperials should feel like looking at themselves five years ago; (b) the community post-pragmatism should feel like a miniature version of every state that ever formed.
- Brother Osric is the encounter's key NPC for the conversion path — proof of concept that the transformation is real and possible.
- The monks' fear is not of the soldiers but of themselves — the old instincts returning, the realization that their transformation may be incomplete. One monk who hasn't slept in a week because he keeps inventorying the tool shed for improvised weapons and *can't stop doing it*.
- The hospitality violation cuts both ways: the monks refused aid to desperate people (violating their own principles); the deserters refused the threshold terms (making genuine hospitality impossible). Neither side can claim clean hands in how this started.
